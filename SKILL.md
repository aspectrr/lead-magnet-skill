# Lead Magnet Generator

> Design a Hormozi-style lead magnet — a complete solution to one narrow problem that earns the right to sell the core offer — for any business.

## When To Use

- User wants to capture leads (email, phone, booked call, follower) for a business, product, or offer.
- User asks for a "freebie," "lead magnet," "opt-in," "free resource," "lead-gen asset," or "magnet offer."
- An ad campaign or landing page needs something to trade for attention because the core offer is too expensive to lead with cold.
- User is rebuilding a funnel that gets traffic but no opt-ins (the magnet — or its packaging — is the likely constraint).
- User wants a lower cost-per-customer: lead with a free/cheap asset, then convert to the paid core offer.

Do **not** use for pure sales scripts, pricing/discount strategy, or paid-ad creative in isolation. This skill produces the asset + the conversion path around it; it assumes the core offer already exists.

## Hormozi's Lead Magnet Framework

Hormozi's central move is the **pretzel at the bar**: give away something salty that solves a small hunger and *creates* a thirst only your paid drink can satisfy. A lead magnet is a **complete solution to one narrow problem** that, once solved, reveals the next problem — the one your core offer solves for money. Lead with a magnet instead of the core offer when the core offer is too big a leap for a cold stranger.

### The 5 Lead Magnet Types

Composed from the book's two dimensions: **3 solution types** × **4 delivery methods** (up to 12 permutations). Treat the five below as the working menu.

**Solution type** — *how the problem gets solved:*
1. **Reveal Their Problem (Diagnosis).** Make an invisible problem visible and quantified. Works best when the problem gets worse with time (slow site losing revenue, posture causing future pain, termites eating the house). The reveal is free; the fix is the core offer.
2. **Samples & Trials.** Give full-but-brief access to the core offer, limited by uses or time. Fits recurring solutions to recurring problems — consumables, software seats, one free adjustment. They feel the win; ongoing benefit requires paying.
3. **One Step of a Multi-Step Process.** Deliver one valuable step of a larger sequence for free; the remaining steps are the core offer. This very book is the example — one step toward $1M/yr profit, the next step is partnering with the author's firm.

**Delivery method** — *the medium the solution arrives in (multiply by the 3 solution types for up to 12 variants of one problem):*
4. **Software** — a calculator, dashboard, audit tool, spreadsheet that does a job for them.
5. **Information** — course, guide, checklist, expert interview, keynote, templates. Anything learnable.
6. **Service** — work done for free: an audit, a free adjustment, running their ads for 30 days, the first coat of sealant applied.
7. **Physical Product** — something they can hold: a supplement sample, a posture chart, a printed book, boxing gloves to attract boxing-gym owners.

**Rule of thumb:** build as many of the 12 variants as you can, rotate them, and let the market pick the winner. Surprises are routine — the magnet you'd bet on often loses to the one you almost didn't ship (the case study that replaced a webinar in Hormozi's origin story).

### The Core Principles

What separates a magnet that converts from one that's ignored:

1. **Narrow problem, not the broad goal.** Solve a small, meaningful slice of the journey toward the customer's big outcome. "Free home valuation," not "sell your house."
2. **It must complete its one job.** A "complete solution to a narrow problem." Half-solutions signal your paid stuff is also half-baked. The magnet should be valuable enough you *could* charge for it.
3. **It reveals the next problem — solvable only by the core offer.** Design the handoff deliberately. If the magnet doesn't expose the core offer's problem, it's a dead end.
4. **The right person, not any person.** Narrow problem + homogeneous audience = a list of people pre-qualified for the core offer. A broad magnet attracts tire-kickers who inflate opt-in numbers and destroy conversion rates.
5. **Make a Grand Slam.** A Grand Slam Offer is one so good people feel stupid saying no. The same logic applies to free magnets — often more so. Out-value every competing free thing in the market. Provide more value than the price of your core offer *before* they've paid a cent.
6. **Give away the secrets, sell the implementation.** Hold nothing back on the free asset. 99% won't buy, but they'll build (or destroy) your reputation based on the free thing. The value you give in advance is the down payment on trust from the 1% who later pay.
7. **Ease of consumption multiplies leads.** The same asset in ebook + audio + video + interactive tool captures 2–4× the leads for the same work. Remove every step between "interested" and "consuming."
8. **The package beats the product.** Headline, image, subheadline — in that order — drive 2–10× engagement. The book's own title was chosen by A/B test, not intuition. "How to get strangers to want to buy your stuff" beat near-identical variants by two words.

### Matching Lead Magnet to Business Type

Pick the solution type from the **shape of the core offer**:

| Core offer shape | Best magnet solution type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One-time expensive purchase (house, retrofit, surgery) | **Reveal Problem** | Stranger won't commit cold; a free diagnosis surfaces urgency and proves expertise. |
| Recurring / consumable (SaaS, supplement, membership, weekly service) | **Sample & Trial** | They feel the benefit on a clock; ongoing value requires staying. |
| Complex multi-step outcome (coaching, done-with-you services, long transformation) | **One Step of Multi-Step** | Give the first valuable step free; the remaining steps are the paid scope. |

Then pick the **delivery method** from the **audience's consumption habit and the cost-to-deliver**:
- B2B / technical audiences → **Software** (calculators, auditors) and **Information** (frameworks, teardowns).
- Consumers with a tangible pain → **Service** (free session) or **Physical Product** (sample).
- Content-native audiences (creators, marketers, founders) → **Information** in multiple formats.

**Audience discipline — homogeneous lists.** The magnet should target one narrowly defined customer solving one narrowly defined problem. A magnet for "gym owners doing under $10k/mo" beats one for "business owners." Each magnet builds a *homogeneous list*: people who share the same narrow problem and therefore convert to the *same* core offer at a higher rate. Multiple magnets → multiple homogeneous lists → multiple targeted conversion paths. Resist the urge to make one magnet serve everyone.

### The "Hook, Retain, Reward" Framework

Three sequential jobs every magnet must do. Think of it as the conversion architecture wrapped around the asset:

1. **HOOK — get them to raise a hand.** The headline + image + subheadline promise a specific, narrow, desirable outcome. This is the Ogilvy "80 cents of your dollar." Test headline first, then image, then subheadline. The hook must name the narrow problem and the specific customer, and imply the speed/specificity of the result ("213 members and $112k revenue to a small San Diego gym").
2. **RETAIN — get them to actually consume it.** Make it complete, make it good (Grand Slam quality), and make it easy (every format, every device, zero friction). A magnet that's claimed but never consumed produces no trust, no revealed problem, and no sale.
3. **REWARD — the CTA that converts consumption to the next step.** Once consumed, some prospects are ready. Give a clear, simple, action-oriented call to action with a *reason to act now*.

**The CTA's two parts:** (a) *what to do* — call now, book the call, reply with the word, click the button — in plain imperative language, never clever; (b) *reason to act now*, because any reason beats no reason. Hormozi's three reason-types:
- **Scarcity** — limited supply vs. demand. Use *real* scarcity (your actual capacity cap, onboarding limit, inventory). Advertise the genuine constraint.
- **Urgency** — limited time. A real deadline, an expiring bonus, a promotion window. Shorten the window to speed action.
- **"Fraternity party planner"** — make up a reason. ("Because… it's my birthday and I want you to celebrate with me.") Any stated reason, even a playful one, lifts response over silence.

## Procedure

Run these six steps in order. Each produces a concrete deliverable the user can act on.

**1. Analyze the business and target audience.**
- State the core offer in one sentence and its price/profit per customer.
- Name the single homogeneous audience whose narrow problem the core offer solves. Get specific (industry, stage, pain, dollars).
- Map the customer's problem-solution cycle: the big outcome they want, and the chain of smaller problems en route to it.
- Identify whether the core offer is a *one-time expensive buy*, a *recurring/consumable*, or a *complex multi-step outcome*. This sets the magnet type.

**2. Select the lead magnet type.**
- Pick the **solution type** from the matching table above.
- Brainstorm **all four delivery methods** applied to that solution (up to 4 variants). Recommend shipping 2–3 and rotating them.
- Write one sentence per candidate: *narrow problem solved → next problem revealed → core offer that solves it.* If the chain breaks, reject the candidate.

**3. Design the hook.**
- Draft 3–5 headline options. Each must name the specific outcome and specific customer. Prefer "How to…" framing (the book's own test showed "how to" beats the imperative form).
- Draft a subheadline and an image direction.
- Specify the A/B test plan: test headline first, then image, then subheadline. If the user has any audience, define the poll/split-test mechanic.

**4. Structure the content/reward.**
- Outline what the magnet delivers — the actual steps, sections, or tool outputs. It must be a *complete* solution to its one narrow problem, not a teaser.
- Flag where the next problem gets revealed and how the core offer is positioned as the fix.
- Specify the formats to ship (text + audio + video + interactive where feasible) to maximize consumption.

**5. Define the conversion path.**
- Map the post-consumption CTA: exact action verb + the reason-to-act-now (scarcity, urgency, or a stated "because…").
- Define the capture mechanic (email gate, booked call, reply-with-a-word) and the follow-up touchpoints that reinforce the revealed problem.
- Note the unit economics: expected delivery cost per magnet, expected lift in engaged leads, and the target cost-per-customer. (A good magnet routinely cuts CAC 3× by pulling 10× more engaged leads per ad dollar.)

**6. Specify the landing page requirements.**
- Above the fold: hook headline, subheadline, image/preview of the magnet, single clear opt-in.
- Capture only what's needed to deliver + follow up.
- Thank-you / consumption page: the asset, in all formats, with the CTA and reason-to-act-now visible immediately.
- Mobile-first; load speed treated as part of the offer.

## Pitfalls

- **Leading with the core offer when a magnet fits.** If the core offer is expensive or a big commitment, cold traffic stalls. Try the core offer first (Hormozi says so) — but if it doesn't engage, switch to a magnet rather than burning more ad budget.
- **Broad problem, broad audience.** A magnet for "everyone" builds a heterogeneous list that converts to nothing. Narrow the problem and the audience together.
- **Teaser, not a complete solution.** A magnet that hints without solving signals your paid work is also shallow. Solve the narrow problem fully.
- **No revealed problem / no handoff.** If consuming the magnet doesn't surface the core offer's problem, you've given a gift with no path to revenue. Design the reveal deliberately.
- **Holding back the secrets.** Fear of "giving too much away" produces fluff that wrecks reputation. Give the real value; sell the implementation.
- **One format only.** Shipping the asset in a single format leaves 2–4× of leads on the table for zero extra insight.
- **Untested packaging.** Guessing the headline instead of testing it. The headline is ~80% of the result; never ship it untested if you have any audience to test against.
- **Vague or absent CTA.** "Learn more" and "don't delay" lose to "call now" + a real reason. Clever loses to clear, every time.
- **Fake scarcity/urgency.** Invented limits get caught and corrode trust. Use your genuine capacity, inventory, or calendar constraints.
- **Measuring opt-ins instead of customers.** Opt-in count is vanity; cost-per-customer is the score. A magnet that floods unqualified leads raises apparent success while hurting unit economics.
- **Stopping at one magnet.** Twelve variants exist per problem. The first winner is rarely the best — rotate and let data decide.

## Verification

A lead magnet design passes when it satisfies all of the following. State each as Y/N with evidence.

1. **Narrow problem?** Can you name the one specific problem in a single sentence, scoped to one homogeneous audience? (Not the broad goal.)
2. **Complete solution?** Does it fully solve that narrow problem on its own — valuable enough you could charge for it?
3. **Reveals the next problem?** After consuming it, is the customer *more* aware of a problem only the core offer solves? Name the reveal point explicitly.
4. **Type match?** Does the solution type fit the core-offer shape (one-time → Reveal; recurring → Sample; multi-step → One Step)?
5. **Homogeneous list?** Will the opt-ins be people qualified for the *same* core offer? Or is the audience too broad?
6. **Grand Slam quality?** Does it out-value every competing free asset in the niche?
7. **Multi-format?** Is it specified in at least the formats the audience actually consumes?
8. **Tested hook?** Is there a headline/image/subheadline A/B plan, with the headline tested first?
9. **Clear CTA + reason?** Is the post-consumption call to action a plain imperative with a real scarcity/urgency/"because" reason?
10. **Unit economics sane?** Does delivery cost + ad cost per engaged lead beat leading with the core offer alone on cost-per-customer? Show the math.

If any answer is N, fix it before shipping. A magnet that fails 1–4 is structurally broken; one that fails 5–10 is fixable in iteration.
